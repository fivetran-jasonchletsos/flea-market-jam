Identifying Cobalt Blue Glass

What it is

Cobalt glass is deep blue colored with cobalt oxide. It can look almost black where it is thick and show clear blue against light. Lighter blues, such as aqua and turquoise, come from other colorants. Do not call every blue piece cobalt in a listing.

Makers and marks

  • Anchor Hocking. The mark is an anchor over the letter H.
  • Hazel-Atlas. The mark is usually the letter H over the letter A. Look at the whole mark, since it is easy to confuse with the Anchor Hocking mark.
  • Fire-King. Ovenware from the 1940s on, often marked Fire-King. Its blue is usually a lighter shade called Sapphire, not true cobalt.
  • Fenton, Cambridge, Blenko. Many pieces carry no mark, or only a paper label that has fallen off. Check each maker against its collector club or a museum page before you name it.

Mark meanings change by era. Match any mark you find against a trusted reference before you put a maker in a listing.

Old or reproduction

  • Mold seams. A thin ridge shows the piece was made in a mold. Pressed glass often has seams up the sides and across the base. Corning Museum of Glass explains them in its glass dictionary.
  • Pontil mark. A rough scar on the base where the piece was broken off the rod. It means the piece was blown. It does not prove age, since modern art glass has them too.
  • Weight and color. Compare with a piece you know is old. Reproductions are often lighter, thinner and too even in color.
  • Wear. Age shows as fine scratches on the base from sliding and small nicks on the rim. Even, perfect wear in odd places is a warning sign.
  • Sharp mold detail on a piece sold as old is a warning sign.

Photos to take

The whole base straight on, every mark in angled light, the seams, the rim, any chips or cloudy patches, and a close-up of the pattern.

The UV light test

Uranium glass glows a bright neon green under UV light and usually looks yellow or green in normal light. Glass with manganese glows a weaker yellow-green. Cobalt blue glass is not uranium glass, so do not expect a green glow from it. Never look into a UV light, and use UV-blocking glasses. See the Oak Ridge museum page on uranium glass.

Check before you rely on this. Check current health and radiation guidance before you handle or sell uranium glass. Do not eat or drink from suspect pieces, and do not grind or sand glass. Older glassware can also contain lead, so do not claim a piece is food-safe.
