Identifying Jewelry

Common stamps

StampUsually means
925 or STERLINGSilver, 92.5 percent pure
10K, 14K, 18KSolid gold, by parts out of 24
585 and 750European gold marks, about 14K and 18K
PLAT or 950Platinum
GF, RGP, GP, HGEGold filled, rolled gold plate, gold plate, heavy gold electroplate. These are not solid gold.

A stamp is a claim, not proof. A stamp can be fake, and a photo cannot confirm what the metal is. Describe what the piece says, and say whether you tested it.

Rules on describing metal

The Federal Trade Commission has rules for how you can describe gold, silver and platinum, and for terms such as gold filled and gold plated. Read them before you list: 16 CFR Part 23. The safe habit is to repeat what the mark says and never claim more purity than it states.

How to test

  • Magnet. Gold, silver and platinum are not magnetic. If a strong magnet grabs it, it is not those metals. If it does not, the piece could still be plated or base metal.
  • Acid kits. They scratch the surface, so test a hidden spot, and never on a collectible piece.
  • XRF machine. A jeweler or pawn shop can read the metal without damage. This is the best check for a valuable piece.
  • Plating wear. Brassy color on high points means plating.

Costume jewelry that sells

Marked pieces from makers such as Trifari, Coro, Miriam Haskell, Eisenberg and Weiss are collectible. Marked pieces can sell from tens of dollars into the hundreds, and rare ones for more. Check sold listings for the exact piece, since prices vary widely. Some makers did not sign early pieces.

Stones

A clear stone can be a natural diamond, a lab-grown diamond or a lookalike, and you cannot tell by eye. If you cannot test it, do not call it a diamond. Write "clear stone, untested."

Photos to take

Every stamp in a close-up with good light, the clasp, the back of each stone setting, the full piece, a ruler for scale, and any damage.
